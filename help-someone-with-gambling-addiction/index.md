# Someone you love is gambling

By [Kyrylo Lozovyi](https://trysilex.com/about/) · August 10, 2026

**Short answer:** Three things, in this order. **Protect the essential money first** — rent, utilities, food — before anything else, because that is the damage that compounds fastest. **Don't clear their debt.** Repeatedly covering losses removes the consequence and reliably makes it worse. And **get support for yourself**, today, without waiting for them to be ready — you don't need their permission, and helplines take calls from family members.

Most guidance written for you is about them — how to spot it, how to persuade them, how to get them into treatment. This page starts somewhere else, because in the first week the thing that actually determines how bad this gets is not whether they're ready to stop. It's whether the household still has rent money in a month.

## First: protect the essential money

Do this before the conversation, before the research, before anything. Not as punishment — as damage control while the rest gets worked out.

- **List what must be paid over the next 30 days:** housing, utilities, food, transport, childcare, medication, minimum debt payments.
- **Move that money somewhere they can't reach** — an account in your name only. This is the single highest-value action available to you.
- **Check what's joint.** Joint accounts, joint credit, overdrafts, savings, and anything with your name on it that they can borrow against. If they can reach it, assume at some point it will be reached.
- **Watch for credit taken in your name.** Check your own credit file. This is an unpleasant thing to do to someone you love and it is unfortunately common enough to be worth doing.
- **Don't lend, don't cover, don't top up.** Including small amounts. Including "just this once, so the kids don't notice."

If money is already short, free debt advice is available to *you*, not only to them — [the options are here](https://trysilex.com/gambling-debt-help/).

## Why clearing the debt backfires

It is the most natural thing in the world to want to make it go away, and it is the move that most reliably makes the next round bigger. Two reasons:

The consequence is doing work. A debt that gets absorbed by someone else teaches that losses are survivable and someone will catch them, which is precisely the belief that funds the next session. And a cleared balance is, in practice, a fresh line of credit — the most common thing that happens after a bailout is a return to gambling with more room to lose.

This is not a rule about being harsh. Protect the household, protect the children, protect yourself. Just don't route money to the operators through your own account.

## The conversation

You probably won't get one clean, decisive talk, and expecting one sets you up to feel it failed. What tends to work better:

- **Pick a calm, sober, private moment.** Not right after a discovery, not during a match, not at midnight.
- **Describe what you've seen, not what they are.** "£800 left the account in three days and I don't know where it went" is hard to argue with. "You're a compulsive gambler" starts a different, unwinnable conversation.
- **Expect lying, and don't take it as a verdict on the relationship.** Concealment is close to a defining feature of a gambling problem, and shame drives it. Someone lying about this is usually not doing it to you so much as to themselves.
- **Say what you're doing regardless of their answer.** "I'm moving the bill money" doesn't require agreement.
- **Offer a next step, not a diagnosis.** A helpline number, one free assessment, or [a nine-question self-check](https://trysilex.com/gambling-self-check/) they can take privately without telling anyone the result.

Don't issue a threat you're not going to carry out. One boundary you keep is worth more than five you don't.

## If they are ready to act

The things that work are the same regardless of who's asking, and they work better when the person sets them up themselves:

- [Self-exclusion](https://trysilex.com/self-exclusion-guide/) — free, and the only layer they can't reverse in a craving.
- [A bank gambling block](https://trysilex.com/block-gambling-transactions-bank/) — free, and takes days to lift rather than seconds.
- [Blocking on the phone](https://trysilex.com/block-gambling-sites-iphone/) — and if you're holding a Screen Time passcode for them, that's a real, useful role. Just be aware of what it makes you: the person who has to say no at 1am. It works better with a friend than a partner.

## Get support for yourself. Now, not later

You do not have to wait for them to be ready, and you do not need their permission.

- **Gam-Anon** — peer support specifically for families and partners of gamblers.
- **National helplines take your call too.** US: [1-800-522-4700](tel:18005224700), 24/7. UK: GamCare's National Gambling Helpline, [0808 8020 133](tel:08088020133). You are allowed to ring them about someone else, and they will not tell you to leave.
- **Free debt advice** — [StepChange](https://www.stepchange.org) and [National Debtline](https://nationaldebtline.org) in the UK, [NFCC](https://www.nfcc.org) members in the US.
- **Your own therapy**, if you can get it. Living with this is its own injury, separate from theirs.

## Two things worth knowing

**Gambling harm and suicide risk travel together.** If they've said anything that worries you, take it seriously and say so out loud — asking directly does not plant the idea. In the US, 988 answers 24/7; in the UK, Samaritans on 116 123.

**None of this is your fault, and you can't do it for them.** You can make the environment safer, hold a boundary, and be there when they're ready. What you can't do is want it more than they do — and burning yourself out trying is a real risk, not a hypothetical one.

## Common questions

### Should I pay off my partner's gambling debt?

Almost always no, however much you want the problem gone. Covering losses softens the consequence the person is meant to be feeling, and the pattern usually repeats at a larger scale. It also puts you in the position of being the thing standing between them and reality, which damages the relationship in a way that is hard to undo. Free debt advisers will help you separate protecting the household from bailing out the gambling.

### How do I know if it's really a gambling problem?

Secrecy and lying about money are the most common early signals — money missing without explanation, unexplained transfers, defensiveness about phone use, borrowing that doesn't add up. The formal screener is the PGSI, but it's self-report, so it only helps if they take it themselves and answer honestly.

### Can I make them stop?

No, and the attempt tends to make it more hidden. What you can control is the environment: household money, your own accounts, what you fund. Blocks and self-exclusion are far more effective when the person sets them up themselves, but you can put your own finances out of reach either way.

### Should I give them an ultimatum?

Threats you won't follow through on cost you credibility and teach them the boundary isn't real. Clear, specific and kept beats dramatic. "I'm moving the bill money into an account in my name" is a boundary. "I'll leave if it happens again" is only a boundary if you'll do it.

### Where can family members get help?

Gam-Anon runs peer support specifically for families and partners of gamblers. National helplines take calls from affected others as well as gamblers — in the US, 1-800-522-4700; in the UK, GamCare's National Gambling Helpline on 0808 8020 133. None of it requires the gambler's involvement or consent.

## If you need someone right now

- US: [1-800-GAMBLER](tel:18004262537) — National Problem Gambling Helpline — free, confidential, 24/7
- US: [988](tel:988) — Suicide & Crisis Lifeline — call or text if it feels like too much
- UK: [0808 8020 133](tel:08088020133) — National Gambling Helpline (GamCare) — free, confidential, 24/7
- UK: [116 123](tel:116123) — Samaritans
- Elsewhere: [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com) — Crisis lines for your country
