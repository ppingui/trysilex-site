The most useful thing anyone told me about cravings is that they are shaped like waves, not like switches. A switch stays on until something turns it off. A wave rises, crests and falls on its own schedule, and there is nothing you have to do to make the fall happen.

The single urge: roughly 20 to 30 minutes

An individual gambling urge typically peaks and subsides within about twenty to thirty minutes. It is the figure used throughout clinical self-help material on cravings, and the point of it is not precision — it is that the number is small and finite. You are not being asked to resist forever. You are being asked to be somewhere else for half an hour.

Two things distort the estimate while you're in it:

  • Clusters. One urge that ends and restarts twice feels like a ninety-minute urge. It isn't; it's three waves and two gaps you didn't notice.
  • Cue exposure. If the trigger stays in front of you — the app icon, the match on TV, the promo email — the wave keeps getting re-launched. This is why removing cues matters more than resisting them: block the access and get off the marketing lists.

The first weeks: worse before better

Frequency is usually highest in the first one to two weeks, then declines unevenly. Intensity tends to ease across roughly the first three months — which is why 90 days is a common frame for structured programs, including the one in Silex.

Expect the curve to be lumpy rather than smooth. Predictable spikes cluster around:

  • Money arriving — payday, a bonus, a tax refund.
  • Fixtures and events — the start of a season, a final, a big race.
  • The old time slot — if you gambled at 11pm, 11pm will ask about it.
  • Emotional states you used to gamble at — boredom, loneliness, stress, and, for a lot of people, feeling good and wanting to celebrate.

Anna Lembke's framing in Dopamine Nation is the clearest short explanation of why the early part feels bad: the pleasure and pain systems share a balance, and after heavy stimulation the balance sits tilted towards discomfort for a while before it resets. Knowing that the low mood in week one is a recovery signal rather than evidence you have ruined yourself makes it much easier to sit through.

Why betting doesn't end the urge

It feels like it does, and that is the trap. The relief that arrives when you place a bet is mostly the removal of the discomfort the craving created — not a gain, just a return to baseline. Then the reward system draws its conclusion: urges get answered. Next time it asks louder and sooner.

The reverse is also true and much less discussed: every urge you ride out is evidence in the other direction. It is not willpower being spent; it's a pattern being unlearned. That is why the tenth wave is genuinely easier than the first — not because you got tougher, but because the prediction changed.

Urge surfing: what to actually do for 30 minutes

Urge surfing comes from relapse-prevention work (G. Alan Marlatt and colleagues) and it deliberately avoids both fighting the craving and obeying it:

  1. Name it. "This is an urge. It's about a 7." Rating it converts a flood into an observation, which is a smaller thing.
  2. Slow your breathing. Four in, four hold, four out, four hold, for a few minutes. It won't remove the craving; it takes the alarm response down enough to think.
  3. Find it in your body. Chest, stomach, hands, jaw. Notice whether it is rising or falling right now. Cravings feel infinite in the abstract and specific when inspected.
  4. Set a timer and stay busy. Twenty minutes, doing something that occupies hands and eyes — a walk, a shower, washing up, anything physical. Not sitting still with your phone in your hand.
  5. Re-read why you stopped. Your own words from a calm day beat any argument you can construct at peak craving.
  6. Tell someone, if there is someone. "I'm having a bad one" is a complete sentence, and saying it out loud reliably takes the top off it.

What the pattern means for how you plan

If urges are short and self-limiting, then the whole game is surviving thirty minutes without access. That reframes what a blocker is for. It doesn't have to be permanent or unbreakable — it has to outlast half an hour:

  • A DNS-level block buys minutes.
  • A bank block with a 48-hour cooling-off buys two days.
  • Self-exclusion buys months or years.

Stack them and the wave has nowhere to land. And if one does land — what you do in the hour afterwards matters far more than the slip itself.
