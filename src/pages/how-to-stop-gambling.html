Almost every guide to quitting gambling is a list of tips that assume the reader will be rational at the moment of decision. The whole problem is that you won't be. So this plan is built the other way round: make the decisions now, while you're calm, that remove the need to decide later.

Step 1 — Barriers that don't depend on how you feel

Do these three today. Together they take about half an hour and cost nothing, and they are ranked by how hard they are to undo, strongest first.

  1. Self-exclude. In the UK, one free GAMSTOP registration covers every UK-licensed online operator for 6 months, 1 year or 5 years. In the US, register with your state programme and with each sportsbook individually. This is the only layer you genuinely cannot reverse in a craving.
  2. Get your bank to decline gambling payments. Most UK banks offer this in-app with a 48–72 hour cooling-off before it lifts; Monzo lets you set up to 12 months. In the US, ask your issuer to block merchant category code 7995.
  3. Block it on your phone. A device-wide DNS profile covers every app and browser on Wi-Fi and cellular; Screen Time stops reinstalls if someone else holds the passcode. Which blocker to use →

Then close the two routes people forget: unsubscribe from every gambling email and turn off every push notification, and unlink e-wallets and prepaid cards from operator accounts. Sportsbook marketing is timed and targeted; it is not neutral information arriving at a random moment.

Step 2 — Learn what an urge actually does

A gambling urge typically peaks and passes within 20 to 30 minutes, and it passes whether or not you bet. Betting doesn't end an urge — it teaches your brain that urges get answered, which makes the next one arrive sooner and louder.

So the skill is narrow and learnable: be somewhere else for half an hour. Rate the urge out of ten, slow your breathing, notice where it sits in your body, set a timer, do something physical, re-read your own reasons for stopping, and tell someone if there is someone to tell.

Step 3 — Replace what gambling was doing for you

This is the step that gets skipped, and the reason people relapse at week six with all their blocks still switched on. Gambling was doing a job. Until something else does that job, the vacancy stays open.

Work out which one it was, because the replacement differs:

What gambling gave youWhat tends to actually fill it
Escape — switching your head off Anything genuinely absorbing and low-stakes: exercise, games, a craft, long walks. The requirement is absorption, not virtue.
The rush — action, stakes, adrenaline Real stakes with real recovery: sport, competition, climbing, performing, anything with a scoreboard that isn't money.
Chasing — winning back what you lost A concrete debt plan, so the hole has a route out that isn't a bet. Free advice from StepChange, National Debtline or an NFCC member.
Company — the app was always there People, on a schedule you don't have to feel motivated to keep: a group, a regular game, a standing call.

This is why 90 days is the usual frame for structured programs: it's roughly how long it takes for the urges to thin out and for something else to be in place.

Get a human involved

Not a substitute for the above — a multiplier on it. All of these are free:

  • A national helpline. They take calls from people who are nowhere near ready to quit, and from family members. Numbers are at the bottom of this page.
  • Gamblers Anonymous or SMART Recovery. Two very different traditions — one fellowship-based, one cognitive-behavioural and secular. Try both before deciding groups aren't for you.
  • Treatment. CBT for gambling disorder has the strongest evidence base of any psychological treatment here. In the UK it is available on the NHS through the National Gambling Support Network; in the US, coverage varies by state and by insurer, and your state council on problem gambling can point you at what's local.
  • Free debt advice, if money is the immediate pressure. Debt and chasing feed each other, and dealing with one makes the other much easier.

What doesn't work

  • Deciding to be more disciplined. Not because you're weak — because gambling products are engineered to defeat exactly that. Variable rewards, near-misses and losses dressed as wins are design decisions, documented in detail in Natasha Dow Schüll's Addiction by Design.
  • Keeping one account "just for the big games". This is the single most common way a serious attempt ends.
  • A blocker as your only move. One minute to remove, and no plan behind it.
  • Secrecy. Hiding it is the mechanism that lets it grow. Telling one person is the highest-leverage unpleasant thing on this page.
  • Waiting for rock bottom. There isn't a floor that arrives and fixes this. People stop from wherever they happen to be standing.

Where to start, if you only do one thing

Take the 9-question PGSI self-check — two minutes, nothing stored — and then do whichever barrier in Step 1 you're most reluctant about. The reluctance is usually pointing at the one that would work.

And if you've already slipped: the next hour matters more than the slip does.
